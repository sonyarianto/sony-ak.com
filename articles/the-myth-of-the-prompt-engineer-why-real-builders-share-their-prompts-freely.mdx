<p>In the fast-moving world of AI development, there's a quiet misunderstanding spreading among some practitioners: that the value of a <em>prompt engineer</em> lies in their collection of “secret, high-performing prompts.”</p><p>Let’s be clear: <strong>real engineers don’t hoard prompts</strong>. They <strong>share</strong>, they <strong>iterate</strong>, and they understand that <strong>prompting is not a product—it's a disposable tool.</strong></p><h3 id="prompts-have-a-short-shelf-life"><strong>Prompts Have a Short Shelf Life</strong></h3><p>What worked amazingly well last week might break next month. Why?</p><ul><li><strong>LLMs are evolving fast.</strong> Every few weeks, new models launch, existing ones get updates, and the behavior of your favorite model can shift without notice.</li><li><strong>Prompts are tightly coupled to model behavior.</strong> A prompt fine-tuned for GPT-4 might behave erratically on Claude, and even GPT-4’s own behavior can change subtly over time.</li><li><strong>Alignment priorities change.</strong> A lab might decide to make a model safer, more concise, or more factual—each decision alters how it interprets the same prompt.</li></ul><p>We’ve seen it ourselves. When our team upgraded from Gemini 1.5 to 2.0, <strong>it took more than a week to realign all our prompts</strong> so that our workflows behaved like they used to. That’s not poor engineering. That’s the nature of prompt-based development.</p><h3 id="your-value-isn%E2%80%99t-in-the-prompt"><strong>Your Value Isn’t in the Prompt</strong></h3><p>If you think your value is tied to a specific prompt you wrote, that’s a red flag—not of competence, but of <strong>insecurity</strong>.</p><p>Because here’s the truth: <strong>writing a good prompt is easy.</strong> What’s hard is:</p><ul><li>Understanding the underlying problem deeply.</li><li>Knowing which model to use (or not use at all).</li><li>Realizing when a prompt is the wrong tool, and shifting to code, retrieval, APIs, or rules-based logic instead.</li><li>Navigating fallback strategies when LLM output becomes unpredictable.</li></ul><p><strong>Prompting is a tool. Not a role. Not a job. Not a moat.</strong></p><p>Engineers worth their salt are not defined by the syntax of a prompt—they’re defined by their ability to build solutions that work.</p><h3 id="prompting-is-not-sacred"><strong>Prompting Is Not Sacred</strong></h3><p>Because prompting is just one tool in the <strong>problem-solving toolbox</strong>, we should treat it like any other disposable piece of logic:</p><ul><li>You write it,</li><li>You test it,</li><li>You throw it away when it stops working.</li></ul><p>Think of it like CSS: nobody protects their CSS like intellectual property, because the <strong>value is in the outcome</strong>, not the rule you wrote.</p><h3 id="why-you-should-share-your-prompts"><strong>Why You Should Share Your Prompts</strong></h3><p>If your prompts work well right now—<strong>share them</strong>.</p><ul><li>They’ll likely break soon anyway.</li><li>You might get feedback that makes them better.</li><li>Someone else might iterate on top and spark new insights you hadn’t considered.</li><li>And most importantly, it shows you’re confident in your ability to solve, not just instruct.</li></ul><p>In fact, <strong>sharing is the best way to get better.</strong> Communities thrive on openness. The most respected builders aren't the ones who hide—they're the ones who teach.</p><h3 id="finally"><strong>Finally</strong></h3><p>If you still think “prompt engineer” is a job title worth protecting, <strong>you might be focusing on the wrong game</strong>. The future doesn’t belong to prompt whisperers—it belongs to <strong>problem solvers</strong> who can use <em>any</em> tool, <em>including</em> prompts, to get the job done.</p><p>So stop hiding your prompts. <strong>They’re not treasure. They’re just tools.</strong></p><p>And tools are meant to be shared.</p><h3 id="support-us">Support Us</h3>
