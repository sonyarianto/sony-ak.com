<p>If you've ever switched languages on a website or app, you've likely seen short codes like "en" for English or "fr" for French. These are called <strong>ISO language codes</strong>, and they play a vital role in ensuring that every language is identified uniquely. But there's another standard at play when you see combinations like "en-US" — that's the <strong>IETF BCP 47</strong> standard. This guide will help you understand both <strong>ISO language codes</strong> and <strong>IETF language tags</strong>, and why they matter.</p><h3 id="what-are-iso-language-codes">What Are ISO Language Codes?</h3><p>ISO language codes are part of the <strong>ISO 639</strong> standard, which provides a set of codes to represent languages. This standard is divided into different parts to cater to different needs:</p><ul><li><strong>ISO 639-1</strong>: The most commonly used, it consists of <strong>two-letter codes</strong> like <strong>"en"</strong> for English and <strong>"id"</strong> for Indonesian. These codes are widely used in apps, websites, and software where the most common languages need to be represented in a compact format.</li><li><strong>ISO 639-2</strong>: This expands the code list to <strong>three-letter codes</strong>, such as <strong>"eng"</strong> for English or <strong>"ind"</strong> for Indonesian. It includes a wider variety of languages and is often used in libraries, databases, and by researchers dealing with more specific language information.</li><li><strong>ISO 639-3</strong>: The most comprehensive of all, it covers <strong>every known language</strong> using three-letter codes, even those spoken by small communities. It’s particularly useful in linguistic research and academic settings.</li></ul><p>These codes ensure that every language, from widely spoken ones to obscure dialects, is represented in a standardized and unique way.</p><h3 id="the-ietf-bcp-47-standard-language-tags-for-regional-variations">The IETF BCP 47 Standard: Language Tags for Regional Variations</h3><p>While ISO codes identify languages, the <strong>IETF BCP 47</strong> standard goes a step further by allowing us to specify <strong>regional or dialectical variations</strong> of a language. These <strong>language tags</strong> combine the <strong>language code</strong> from ISO 639 with a <strong>region code</strong> from the <strong>ISO 3166-1</strong> standard, which represents countries.</p><p>For example:</p><ul><li><strong>"en-US"</strong> stands for <strong>English as spoken in the United States</strong>.</li><li><strong>"en-GB"</strong> represents <strong>English as spoken in the United Kingdom</strong>.</li><li><strong>"id-ID"</strong> means <strong>Indonesian as spoken in Indonesia</strong>.</li></ul><p>Here’s how it works:</p><ul><li><strong>Language code</strong>: The part before the hyphen, like "en" or "id," represents the base language. These come from ISO 639.</li><li><strong>Region code</strong>: The part after the hyphen, like "US" for the United States or "ID" for Indonesia, comes from ISO 3166 and refers to the country or region.</li></ul><h3 id="why-use-language-tags">Why Use Language Tags?</h3><p>Using <strong>IETF BCP 47 language tags</strong> allows systems to be <strong>region-aware</strong>. While "en" refers to English in general, "en-US" and "en-GB" help specify whether the content should use <strong>American English</strong> or <strong>British English</strong>. This matters for differences in <strong>spelling</strong>, <strong>date formatting</strong>, and even <strong>terminology</strong>. For example:</p><ul><li><strong>"color"</strong> in American English vs. <strong>"colour"</strong> in British English.</li><li>The date <strong>"12/31/2024"</strong> (December 31st) in the US would be <strong>"31/12/2024"</strong> in the UK.</li></ul><p>By combining language and region codes, IETF BCP 47 ensures that users get a <strong>localized experience</strong> that feels natural to them, based on where they are or the language they speak.</p><h3 id="are-language-codes-and-tags-unique">Are Language Codes and Tags Unique?</h3><p>Yes, both ISO language codes and IETF language tags are designed to be <strong>unique</strong> within their context:</p><ul><li><strong>ISO 639 codes</strong> ensure that no two languages share the same code. For example, "en" will always refer to English.</li><li><strong>IETF BCP 47 tags</strong> are also unique because they combine language codes and region codes in specific ways, like "en-US" or "fr-CA" (French in Canada).</li></ul><p>This ensures that systems can identify languages and their regional variations <strong>precisely</strong> without any overlap.</p><h3 id="other-points-you-should-know">Other Points You Should Know</h3><p>In addition to the language and region, <strong>IETF BCP 47</strong> tags can also include <strong>optional subtags</strong> for things like:</p><ul><li><strong>Script</strong>: To indicate a specific script used to write the language. For example, <strong>"zh-Hans"</strong> refers to <strong>Simplified Chinese</strong>.</li><li><strong>Variant</strong>: To indicate different versions or dialects of a language. For example, <strong>"sl-rozaj"</strong> refers to the <strong>Resian dialect</strong> of Slovenian.</li></ul><p>These subtags allow for even more <strong>granular control</strong> over language localization, ensuring that content is displayed correctly for users with very specific linguistic needs.</p><h3 id="when-should-you-use-these-codes-and-tags">When Should You Use These Codes and Tags?</h3><ul><li><strong>Developing Multilingual Applications</strong>: If you're building an app that supports multiple languages and regions, using both <strong>ISO codes</strong> and <strong>IETF tags</strong> ensures that users get the right content for their language and region.</li><li><strong>Data Exchange</strong>: In databases or APIs where you need to pass language data between systems, these standardized codes help avoid confusion.</li><li><strong>Localization</strong>: If you're localizing content for different regions, <strong>IETF tags</strong> help ensure that the right <strong>regional variations</strong> of the language are used.</li></ul><h3 id="finally">Finally</h3><p>Both <strong>ISO language codes</strong> and <strong>IETF BCP 47 language tags</strong> are essential tools for managing languages in a globalized world. While ISO codes give us a clear way to identify languages, IETF tags help us <strong>fine-tune</strong> the experience by taking regional and dialectal differences into account.</p><p>The next time you encounter codes like <strong>"en-US" </strong>or <strong>"fr-CA"</strong> you’ll know they’re not just random combinations. They represent a well-structured system to ensure that users get the right language and regional experience, making everything from browsing websites to using apps feel more natural and personalized.</p><h3 id="support-us">Support Us</h3>
