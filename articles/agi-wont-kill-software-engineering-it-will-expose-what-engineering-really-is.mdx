<p>Every few years, software engineers are told—confidently—that <em>this time</em> is different.<br>That the next abstraction, the next framework, the next breakthrough will finally make us obsolete.</p><p>AGI is simply the latest version of that story.</p><p>The anxiety is understandable. Today’s AI models write code faster than most humans ever could. They refactor, debug, reason, and explain. If you freeze the world at <em>code generation</em>, the conclusion feels obvious: fewer engineers will be needed.</p><p>But that framing is deeply misleading.</p><p>The uncomfortable truth is this: <strong>software engineering has never primarily been about writing code</strong>. And AGI, far from eliminating developers, is going to make that fact impossible to ignore.</p><hr><h2 id="the-agi-definition-problem-and-why-it-matters"><strong>The AGI Definition Problem (and Why It Matters)</strong></h2><p>Before we even talk about jobs, we need to address a fundamental issue: <strong>no one agrees on what AGI is</strong>.</p><p>Depending on who you ask, AGI means:</p><ul><li>A system that can pass any cognitive benchmark</li><li>A system that can do most economically useful work</li><li>A system that can do <em>literally anything a human can do</em></li><li>Or simply “whatever today’s models can’t yet do”</li></ul><p>The goalposts move because intelligence isn’t one thing. It’s a collection of abilities—reasoning, judgment, social awareness, accountability, creativity, long-term planning—each of which matures at a different rate.</p><p>By some narrow definitions, <strong>we already crossed the AGI line</strong>. If you showed today’s models to a working engineer in 2015, they would have thought the future had arrived.</p><p>But job displacement arguments rely on a <em>much stronger</em> definition: not “can generate code,” but “can replace a human in a messy organization, over time, with consequences.”</p><p>That is a very different bar.</p><hr><h2 id="most-companies-are-not-bottlenecked-by-code"><strong>Most Companies Are Not Bottlenecked by Code</strong></h2><p>One of the biggest blind spots in AGI discourse is <strong>where software is actually built</strong>.</p><p>Yes, elite tech companies exist. But a massive portion of the global economy looks nothing like Silicon Valley:</p><ul><li>Legacy systems (COBOL is not a meme—it’s still alive)</li><li>Slow release cycles</li><li>Risk-averse cultures</li><li>Software treated as “IT,” not a core competency</li><li>Vague requirements and political constraints</li></ul><p>In these environments, <strong>code generation is not the limiting factor</strong>.</p><p>You could give every engineer unlimited access to the best AI models on earth, and you would <em>not</em> see a 100× productivity explosion. Why?</p><p>Because the constraints are organizational:</p><ul><li>unclear ownership</li><li>misaligned incentives</li><li>outdated processes</li><li>fear of breaking production</li><li>lack of product thinking</li></ul><p>AI accelerates output. It does not magically fix dysfunctional systems. In fact, it often <strong>amplifies existing problems</strong>, producing more artifacts faster without improving decision quality.</p><hr><h2 id="writing-code-is-the-easy-part-it-always-was"><strong>Writing Code Is the Easy Part (It Always Was)</strong></h2><p>This is the part that makes people uncomfortable.</p><p><strong>Typing code has never been the hard part of software engineering.</strong><br>We just pretended it was, because it was visible and measurable.</p><p>The hard parts are:</p><ul><li>deciding <em>what</em> to build</li><li>discovering that you’re wrong</li><li>iterating under incomplete information</li><li>negotiating trade-offs between speed, quality, cost, and risk</li><li>aligning with stakeholders who disagree with each other</li><li>maintaining systems long after the excitement is gone</li></ul><p>These are not mechanical tasks. They are judgment-heavy, context-dependent, and deeply human.</p><p>Even with perfect code generation, someone still needs to:</p><ul><li>define the problem</li><li>validate assumptions</li><li>decide when “good enough” is enough</li><li>take responsibility when things fail</li></ul><p>That responsibility does not disappear just because the code was written by a model.</p><hr><h2 id="taste-is-the-scarce-skill-nobody-talks-about"><strong>Taste Is the Scarce Skill Nobody Talks About</strong></h2><p>One of the most overlooked skills in engineering is <strong>taste</strong>.</p><p>Taste is the ability to:</p><ul><li>recognize when something feels wrong</li><li>distinguish elegance from cleverness</li><li>choose simplicity over feature accumulation</li><li>know when not to build something</li></ul><p>AI is excellent at generating <em>plausible</em> solutions. But plausibility is not quality.</p><p>Quality emerges from experience:</p><ul><li>having seen systems fail</li><li>having maintained bad decisions for years</li><li>having felt the cost of shortcuts</li></ul><p>Taste is not about intelligence alone—it is about <strong>lived context</strong>. And context is expensive to acquire.</p><hr><h2 id="the-engineer-role-will-change-%E2%80%94-that-is-not-the-same-as-disappearing"><strong>The Engineer Role Will Change — That Is Not the Same as Disappearing</strong></h2><p>It is true that the traditional image of an engineer “punching every key by hand” is fading. But this has happened repeatedly:</p><ul><li>We stopped writing assembly.</li><li>We stopped managing memory manually.</li><li>We stopped reimplementing common data structures.</li></ul><p>Each abstraction shift reduced <em>mechanical effort</em> and increased the value of <em>systems thinking</em>.</p><p>AI is just the next abstraction layer.</p><p>The emerging engineer is less typist, more orchestrator:</p><ul><li>guiding AI tools</li><li>validating outputs</li><li>constraining behavior</li><li>integrating across systems</li><li>understanding failure modes</li></ul><p>This raises expectations. It does not remove the role.</p><hr><h2 id="accountability-is-the-missing-ingredient"><strong>Accountability Is the Missing Ingredient</strong></h2><p>One question rarely asked in AGI debates is simple:</p><p><strong>Who is responsible when things go wrong?</strong></p><p>Software exists inside legal, financial, and social systems. Bugs cause outages. Outages cost money. Money involves liability.</p><p>An AI system does not:</p><ul><li>attend postmortems</li><li>explain decisions to regulators</li><li>absorb blame</li><li>build trust over time</li></ul><p>Until AI systems can meaningfully participate in accountability structures—not just generate artifacts—humans remain necessary.</p><p>And if AGI ever <em>does</em> reach that level, the conversation will no longer be about developer jobs. It will be about how society itself is organized.</p><hr><h2 id="adoption-is-uneven-slow-and-messy"><strong>Adoption Is Uneven, Slow, and Messy</strong></h2><p>Technological capability does not instantly translate into economic reality.</p><p>Most organizations:</p><ul><li>adopt late</li><li>adopt partially</li><li>misuse new tools</li><li>take years to change processes</li></ul><p>As long as:</p><ul><li>banking apps remain fragile</li><li>airline systems remain archaic</li><li>enterprise software remains painful</li><li>“digital transformation” remains a multi-year effort</li></ul><p>…the world is not short of software work. It is short of people who can <strong>navigate complexity under constraint</strong>.</p><hr><h2 id="tools-not-gods-will-define-the-near-future"><strong>Tools, Not Gods, Will Define the Near Future</strong></h2><p>Despite the hype, the next decade is unlikely to be about “AI replacing engineers.”</p><p>It will be about:</p><ul><li>engineers who can use AI effectively replacing those who cannot</li><li>smaller teams shipping better software</li><li>faster iteration cycles</li><li>higher expectations for quality and judgment</li></ul><p>This is a tooling revolution, not a labor extinction event.</p><hr><h2 id="what-you-might-still-be-missing"><strong>What You Might Still Be Missing</strong></h2><p>A few additional considerations often overlooked:</p><ul><li><strong>Maintenance dominates creation</strong>: Most software work is about maintaining existing systems, not building new ones. AI helps, but legacy complexity still rules.</li><li><strong>Trust is built, not generated</strong>: Teams trust people, not tools. Trust takes time.</li><li><strong>Constraints define reality</strong>: Compliance, regulation, security, and data ownership do not disappear with better models.</li><li><strong>Human coordination is the bottleneck</strong>: Meetings, alignment, incentives, and communication remain stubbornly human problems.</li></ul><hr><h2 id="finally-agi-doesn%E2%80%99t-end-engineering-%E2%80%94-it-reveals-it"><strong>Finally: AGI Doesn’t End Engineering — It Reveals It</strong></h2><p>AGI does not threaten software engineers because engineers were never hired to write code.</p><p>They were hired to:</p><ul><li>reduce uncertainty</li><li>make trade-offs</li><li>build systems that survive reality</li></ul><p>AI makes parts of this easier, faster, and cheaper. It also raises the bar for what “good” looks like.</p><p>The role will change. The expectations will rise. The weak abstractions will fall away.</p><p>But until organizations themselves become intelligent—and accountable—<strong>the demand for great engineers will not shrink</strong>.</p><p>It will simply become harder to fake.</p><h3 id="support-us">Support Us</h3>
