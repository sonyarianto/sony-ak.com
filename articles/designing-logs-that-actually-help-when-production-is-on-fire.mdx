<p>Most teams do not design logs.<br>They <em>react</em> to incidents.</p><p>Someone adds a <code>console.log</code>, fixes the bug, and moves on. Over time, production logs become a noisy graveyard of half-sentences, inconsistent formats, missing context, and emotionally-charged messages like:</p><blockquote>“wtf is this even happening”</blockquote><p>Then one day, production goes down at 2:47 AM, and suddenly those logs are all you have.</p><p>This article is about <strong>designing logs intentionally</strong>, so that any engineer—on-call or not—can understand what happened <strong>without opening the source code</strong>.</p><hr><h2 id="logs-are-not-debug-statements"><strong>Logs Are Not Debug Statements</strong></h2><p>This is the first mindset shift.</p><p><strong>Logs are operational tools, not developer notes.</strong><br>They exist to help engineers:</p><ul><li>Debug incidents</li><li>Investigate user issues</li><li>Trace failures across systems</li><li>Measure system health</li></ul><p>If a log message only makes sense to the person who wrote the code, it is <strong>not a useful log</strong>.</p><hr><h2 id="1-use-structured-logging-not-free-text-messages"><strong>1. Use Structured Logging (Not Free-Text Messages)</strong></h2><p>Plain text logs are human-readable but <strong>machine-useless</strong>.</p><p>Structured logs (usually JSON) are <strong>queryable, filterable, and aggregatable</strong>.</p><p><strong>Bad</strong></p><pre><code>"Payment failed for user 4821"
</code></pre><p><strong>Good</strong></p><pre><code class="language-json">{
  "level": "error",
  "event": "payment_failed",
  "user_id": "u_4821",
  "order_id": "ord_99234",
  "error_code": "CARD_DECLINED",
  "request_id": "req_9f2a",
  "duration_ms": 1834
}
</code></pre><p>Now your observability stack can:</p><ul><li>Alert on <code>event = payment_failed</code></li><li>Count failure rates by <code>error_code</code></li><li>Track latency trends using <code>duration_ms</code></li><li>Correlate errors by <code>request_id</code></li></ul><p><strong>Design your logs like database rows, not diary entries.</strong></p><hr><h2 id="2-use-log-levels-correctly-stop-abusing-error"><strong>2. Use Log Levels Correctly (Stop Abusing ERROR)</strong></h2><p>Most teams log everything as <code>ERROR</code>.<br>This destroys signal quality.</p><p>Use levels intentionally:</p><ul><li><strong>INFO</strong> → Expected business events<br>(user logged in, order created, webhook received)</li><li><strong>WARN</strong> → Recoverable problems<br>(timeouts with retries, degraded fallbacks, partial failures)</li><li><strong>ERROR</strong> → Genuine failures needing attention<br>(payment failed, data corruption, dependency outage)</li><li><strong>DEBUG</strong> → Development noise<br>(disabled in production)</li></ul><p><strong>Important:</strong><br>If something is part of normal control flow, it is <strong>not an error</strong>.</p><hr><h2 id="3-logs-without-context-are-almost-useless"><strong>3. Logs Without Context Are Almost Useless</strong></h2><p>A log message alone rarely answers anything.</p><p>Context turns logs into answers.</p><p><strong>Always attach context fields:</strong></p><ul><li><strong>Identifiers</strong> → <code>request_id</code>, <code>user_id</code>, <code>order_id</code>, <code>job_id</code></li><li><strong>Values</strong> → <code>amount</code>, <code>count</code>, <code>size</code></li><li><strong>State</strong> → <code>status</code>, <code>current_step</code>, <code>phase</code></li><li><strong>Errors</strong> → <code>error_code</code>, <code>exception_class</code>, <code>stack_trace</code> (sampled)</li><li><strong>Timing</strong> → <code>duration_ms</code>, <code>queued_ms</code>, <code>retry_after_ms</code></li></ul><p>These answer:</p><ul><li><strong>Who</strong> is affected</li><li><strong>What</strong> failed</li><li><strong>Where</strong> in the flow it happened</li><li><strong>How often</strong> it happens</li><li><strong>How long</strong> it took before failing</li></ul><p>If your logs cannot answer these, they will not help during incidents.</p><hr><h2 id="4-correlation-ids-are-non-negotiable"><strong>4. Correlation IDs Are Non-Negotiable</strong></h2><p>Modern systems are concurrent and distributed.<br>Without correlation IDs, logs become a shuffled deck of unrelated events.</p><p><strong>Generate a unique <code>request_id</code> at every entry point</strong>:</p><ul><li>HTTP requests</li><li>Background jobs</li><li>Message queue consumers</li><li>Webhooks</li></ul><p><strong>Propagate it everywhere:</strong></p><ul><li>Across services</li><li>Across async boundaries</li><li>Across retries</li><li>Into all logs</li></ul><p>Without this, debugging production issues becomes detective work with missing pages.</p><p>If you use distributed tracing (OpenTelemetry), also log:</p><ul><li><strong>trace_id</strong></li><li><strong>span_id</strong></li></ul><hr><h2 id="5-never-log-sensitive-data-ever"><strong>5. Never Log Sensitive Data (Ever)</strong></h2><p>This is both a <strong>security risk</strong> and a <strong>compliance nightmare</strong>.</p><p>Never log:</p><ul><li><strong>Passwords</strong></li><li><strong>Tokens / API keys</strong></li><li><strong>Card numbers</strong></li><li><strong>Raw SQL with user input</strong></li><li><strong>Full request or response payloads</strong></li><li><strong>PII without explicit masking</strong></li></ul><p>Use:</p><ul><li>Masked fields (<code>card_last4</code>)</li><li>Redaction middleware</li><li>Allowlists instead of blocklists</li></ul><p><strong>Logs are often retained longer than databases. Treat them as sensitive infrastructure.</strong></p><hr><h2 id="6-add-service-metadata-critical-in-microservices"><strong>6. Add Service Metadata (Critical in Microservices)</strong></h2><p>Logs without service metadata become useless in distributed systems.</p><p>Include:</p><ul><li><strong>service name</strong></li><li><strong>environment</strong> (prod/staging/dev)</li><li><strong>version or commit SHA</strong></li><li><strong>region / datacenter</strong></li><li><strong>container or pod id</strong></li></ul><p>This allows you to answer:</p><ul><li>“Did this break after the last deploy?”</li><li>“Is this only happening in one region?”</li><li>“Is one pod misbehaving?”</li></ul><hr><h2 id="7-control-noise-with-sampling-and-deduplication"><strong>7. Control Noise with Sampling and Deduplication</strong></h2><p>High-volume logs destroy observability.</p><p>If every successful request logs 10 lines, your signal is buried.</p><p><strong>Best practices:</strong></p><ul><li>Sample repeated errors</li><li>Deduplicate identical stack traces</li><li>Convert high-frequency success logs into <strong>metrics</strong></li><li>Rate-limit spammy logs</li></ul><p>Logs should be <strong>diagnostic tools</strong>, not spam.</p><hr><h2 id="8-log-intent-not-just-outcomes"><strong>8. Log Intent, Not Just Outcomes</strong></h2><p>Most logs say <em>what happened</em>.<br>Good logs explain <strong>why the system chose a path</strong>.</p><p>Example:</p><p><strong>Bad</strong></p><pre><code>"Fallback triggered"
</code></pre><p><strong>Good</strong></p><pre><code class="language-json">{
  "event": "cache_fallback_used",
  "reason": "redis_timeout",
  "timeout_ms": 500,
  "request_id": "req_9f2a"
}
</code></pre><p>This tells future engineers:</p><ul><li>What decision was made</li><li>Why it was made</li><li>Under what conditions</li></ul><hr><h2 id="9-design-logs-for-3-am-debugging"><strong>9. Design Logs for 3 AM Debugging</strong></h2><p>Ask yourself:</p><blockquote>If I were paged right now, would these logs tell me what to do?</blockquote><p>Your logs should answer:</p><ul><li><strong>What broke?</strong></li><li><strong>Who is affected?</strong></li><li><strong>Since when?</strong></li><li><strong>How frequently?</strong></li><li><strong>Is this new?</strong></li><li><strong>Did this correlate with a deploy?</strong></li><li><strong>Is the blast radius growing?</strong></li></ul><p>If logs cannot answer these questions, they are not operational logs.</p><hr><h2 id="10-standardize-a-logging-contract-team-wide"><strong>10. Standardize a Logging Contract (Team-Wide)</strong></h2><p>Without standards, logs decay.</p><p>Define a <strong>minimal logging contract</strong>:</p><p><strong>Required fields</strong></p><ul><li><strong>level</strong></li><li><strong>event</strong></li><li><strong>request_id</strong></li><li><strong>service</strong></li><li><strong>env</strong></li><li><strong>version</strong></li></ul><p><strong>Recommended fields</strong></p><ul><li><strong>user_id</strong></li><li><strong>resource_id</strong></li><li><strong>duration_ms</strong></li><li><strong>error_code</strong></li></ul><p>Enforce this in:</p><ul><li>Code reviews</li><li>Logging wrappers</li><li>Lint rules</li><li>Templates</li></ul><hr><h2 id="11-logs-are-not-metrics-use-both"><strong>11. Logs Are Not Metrics (Use Both)</strong></h2><p>Logs explain <strong>why</strong>.<br>Metrics show <strong>how bad</strong>.</p><p>Use:</p><ul><li>Logs for root cause analysis</li><li>Metrics for alerting and trends</li><li>Traces for flow visualization</li></ul><p>Trying to use logs as metrics leads to:</p><ul><li>Cost explosions</li><li>Slow dashboards</li><li>Missed alerts</li></ul><hr><h2 id="12-design-logs-for-humans-and-machines"><strong>12. Design Logs for Humans and Machines</strong></h2><p>Good logs:</p><ul><li>Are <strong>machine-queryable</strong></li><li>But still <strong>human-readable</strong></li><li>Use stable field names</li><li>Avoid cryptic abbreviations</li><li>Avoid emotional messages</li></ul><p>Your future teammate may be reading your logs at 4 AM.<br>Be kind to that person.</p><hr><h2 id="the-real-goal-of-logging"><strong>The Real Goal of Logging</strong></h2><p>The goal is not to “have logs.”</p><p>The goal is:</p><blockquote>Any engineer can open the logs during an incident and <strong>understand what happened without opening the source code.</strong></blockquote><p>If your logs achieve that, you have done logging correctly.</p><hr><h2 id="finally"><strong>Finally</strong></h2><p>Good logging is not accidental.<br>It is a <strong>design discipline</strong>.</p><p>If your team treats logs as first-class system design, production incidents stop being chaotic and start becoming <strong>diagnosable engineering problems</strong>.</p><p>That is the difference between:</p><ul><li>“Production is down, panic”<br>and</li><li>“We know exactly what broke, why, and where to fix it.”</li></ul><h3 id="support-us">Support Us</h3>
