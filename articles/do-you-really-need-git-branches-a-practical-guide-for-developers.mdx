<p>If you’ve ever wondered whether you should use <strong>Git branches</strong> or just work straight on the <code>main</code> branch, you’re not alone. It’s a common question — especially among solo developers or small teams trying to keep things simple. The short answer? <strong>It depends on your workflow, your team size, and whether CI/CD is involved.</strong></p><p>Here’s a practical, no-nonsense breakdown to help you decide <strong>when branches are worth it, and when they just slow you down.</strong></p><hr><h2 id="solo-developer-let%E2%80%99s-talk-simplicity"><strong>Solo Developer? Let’s Talk Simplicity</strong></h2><p>When you’re working <strong>alone</strong>, your workflow doesn’t need to be as complex as a team’s. Branches can be useful, but they’re not always necessary.</p><p>Ask yourself:</p><h3 id="%F0%9F%91%89-are-you-working-on-multiple-features-or-ideas-at-once">👉 Are you working on multiple features or ideas at once?</h3><ul><li><strong>Yes:</strong> Use branches.<br>Even if you’re solo, it’s helpful to isolate features. You might be building a new UI while experimenting with a database refactor — branches help you avoid mixing them up and let you switch focus easily.</li><li><strong>No:</strong> Don’t bother with branches.<br>If you’re working on <strong>one feature at a time</strong>, and don’t have any CI/CD pipelines watching your <code>main</code> branch, <strong>just commit directly</strong> to <code>main</code>. Branching, merging, deleting — it’s extra overhead that slows down small, focused work.</li></ul><hr><h2 id="team-environment-branches-are-non-negotiable"><strong>Team Environment? Branches Are Non-Negotiable</strong></h2><p>In a team setting, even a small one, branches become essential.</p><h3 id="%F0%9F%91%89-are-multiple-people-working-on-different-tasks">👉 Are multiple people working on different tasks?</h3><ul><li><strong>Yes:</strong> Absolutely use branches.<br>Each developer should work in isolation to prevent merge conflicts, overwrite mistakes, or unexpected behavior showing up in shared code.</li><li><strong>No:</strong> If you're pair-programming or everyone is focused on a very tight scope (rare case), you <strong>might</strong> get away without branches — but it’s still safer to use them.</li></ul><hr><h2 id="cicd-pipelines-make-branching-mandatory"><strong>CI/CD Pipelines Make Branching Mandatory</strong></h2><p>If your <code>main</code> branch is connected to a <strong>CI/CD pipeline</strong> — whether that’s automated tests, staging deployments, or production builds — you must <strong>protect the main branch</strong>.</p><p>Why?</p><p>Because:</p><ul><li>A broken commit could trigger a failed build.</li><li>A bug in production could affect users immediately.</li><li>A half-finished feature could slip through and mess up stability.</li></ul><p>So if you <strong>have CI/CD</strong>, <strong>use branches</strong>. Develop in them, test in them, and only merge when things are clean.</p><hr><h2 id="other-considerations-you-might-be-missing"><strong>Other Considerations You Might Be Missing</strong></h2><h3 id="%F0%9F%9B%A0%EF%B8%8F-using-feature-flags-instead-of-branches">🛠️ <strong>Using feature flags instead of branches?</strong></h3><p>Some teams use <strong>feature toggles</strong> in code to avoid branching, especially when practicing <strong>trunk-based development</strong>. This is advanced and only makes sense with strong discipline and automated testing.</p><h3 id="%F0%9F%A7%AA-do-you-experiment-often">🧪 <strong>Do you experiment often?</strong></h3><p>Branches are also great for experiments — try something wild without worrying you’ll wreck your main codebase.</p><h3 id="%F0%9F%91%A5-do-you-need-code-review">👥 <strong>Do you need code review?</strong></h3><p>Branches make code reviews easier. You open a <strong>pull request</strong>, your teammate reviews it, and then it’s merged. Even solo developers sometimes review their own work after a break using PRs.</p><h3 id="%F0%9F%94%84-do-you-rely-on-version-control-for-backups">🔄 <strong>Do you rely on version control for backups?</strong></h3><p>If you’re pushing to Git just for backup purposes and don’t care about collaboration or CI/CD, <strong>branches may be overkill</strong>.</p><hr><h2 id="%E2%9C%85-tldr-%E2%80%94-when-to-use-git-branches">✅ TL;DR — When to Use Git Branches</h2><ul><li><strong>Working solo, one feature at a time, no CI/CD?</strong><br>→ <strong>No need for branches.</strong> Stay lean and fast.</li><li><strong>Working solo, multiple tracks of work?</strong><br>→ <strong>Use branches</strong> for better isolation.</li><li><strong>Working in a team?</strong><br>→ <strong>Always use branches.</strong> Collaboration requires clean workflows.</li><li><strong>Have CI/CD on <code>main</code>?</strong><br>→ <strong>Definitely use branches.</strong> Protect your production pipeline.</li></ul><hr><h2 id="%F0%9F%92%A1-finally">💡 Finally</h2><p><strong>Git branches are not mandatory.</strong> They’re a <strong>tool</strong>, not a rule. Use them when they solve problems — like isolating work, enabling collaboration, or protecting stability — not just because "everyone does it." Sometimes, especially for solo devs, the fastest path is the simplest one: <strong>just commit to <code>main</code> and move forward</strong>.</p><p>But once complexity creeps in — whether from multiple features, team members, or automation — <strong>branching becomes your best friend.</strong></p><h3 id="support-us">Support Us</h3>
