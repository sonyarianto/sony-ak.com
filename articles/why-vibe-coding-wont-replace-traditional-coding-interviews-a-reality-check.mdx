<p>There’s a growing buzz around the idea of replacing traditional coding interviews with <strong>"vibe coding"</strong>—essentially a one-hour session where a candidate builds a small product or feature while "thinking out loud." Proponents argue that this approach better reveals a candidate’s problem-solving style, creativity, and real-world skills. But does it really <strong>replace</strong> the depth and rigor of traditional coding interviews?</p><p>I don’t think so. And here’s why.</p><h3 id="1-vibe-coding-is-context-dependent-and-finite"><strong>1. Vibe Coding Is Context-Dependent and Finite</strong></h3><p>While <strong>vibe coding</strong> can be a useful supplement, it’s a <strong>finite trend</strong>, not a holistic solution. It offers a quick look into a candidate’s <strong>immediate thinking process</strong>, but it’s highly dependent on the nature of the task and the candidate’s familiarity with the environment. Not every role or product fits into the <strong>“ship fast, break things”</strong> mindset. Many positions require <strong>deep technical knowledge</strong>, careful architecture, and long-term scalability planning that simply <strong>can’t be captured in an hour</strong>.</p><h3 id="2-depth-and-long-term-skills-still-matter"><strong>2. Depth and Long-Term Skills Still Matter</strong></h3><p>In an interview, we’re not just looking for someone who can <strong>quickly prototype something flashy</strong>. We’re looking for someone who can <strong>write maintainable code</strong>, <strong>anticipate edge cases</strong>, and <strong>think about performance and security</strong>. These skills come to light when candidates are given deeper problems that force them to consider the <strong>trade-offs</strong>, <strong>optimizations</strong>, and <strong>engineering principles</strong> that underpin robust software systems.</p><h3 id="3-culture-fit-is-crucial-and-it%E2%80%99s-not-just-about-the-code"><strong>3. Culture Fit Is Crucial, and It’s Not Just About the Code</strong></h3><p>Every organization has its own <strong>culture</strong> and <strong>values</strong>. Some prioritize <strong>speed and experimentation</strong>, while others emphasize <strong>stability and collaboration</strong>. The way you evaluate candidates should reflect what’s important to your company. If you’re a fast-moving startup, maybe <strong>vibe coding</strong> aligns with your need for scrappy builders. But if you’re a large-scale enterprise managing critical infrastructure, you’ll want engineers who think about <strong>compliance, reliability, and long-term maintenance</strong>.</p><p>It’s not about replacing one evaluation style with another—it’s about aligning the process with your <strong>organization’s DNA</strong>.</p><h3 id="4-trends-come-and-go-but-fundamentals-remain"><strong>4. Trends Come and Go, but Fundamentals Remain</strong></h3><p>The <strong>tech industry loves trends</strong>—from <strong>pair programming</strong> to <strong>whiteboard interviews</strong>, from <strong>take-home assignments</strong> to now <strong>vibe coding</strong>. But trends are just that: <strong>temporary shifts in practice</strong>. What remains constant is the need to assess <strong>core competencies</strong>: problem-solving, technical knowledge, communication, adaptability, and the ability to work in a team. <strong>Vibe coding</strong> may offer a fresh lens, but it doesn’t replace the <strong>fundamentals</strong> that hiring managers have relied on for years.</p><h3 id="5-a-balanced-approach-is-key"><strong>5. A Balanced Approach Is Key</strong></h3><p>Rather than throwing out traditional coding interviews in favor of vibe coding, we should think about <strong>integrating the best of both worlds</strong>. A comprehensive evaluation might include:</p><ul><li>A <strong>coding challenge</strong> to assess fundamental problem-solving and technical depth.</li><li>A <strong>system design session</strong> to evaluate architecture skills and trade-off reasoning.</li><li>A <strong>vibe coding/product build segment</strong> to observe how the candidate works under real-world constraints and communicates their thought process.</li><li>A <strong>behavioral interview</strong> to gauge culture fit, collaboration style, and adaptability.</li></ul><p>This approach ensures we’re not just hiring someone who can <strong>impress in an hour</strong>, but someone who can <strong>thrive in the long run</strong>.</p><h3 id="6-other-considerations-you-might-be-missing"><strong>6. Other Considerations You Might Be Missing</strong></h3><ul><li><strong>Candidate Experience:</strong> Overloading interviews with trendy formats can be stressful and might favor extroverts over introverts. Not everyone is comfortable <strong>thinking out loud</strong> while building under pressure.</li><li><strong>Bias Risk:</strong> Vibe coding can inadvertently introduce <strong>bias</strong>. It often favors candidates who are <strong>good performers</strong> or <strong>charismatic communicators</strong>, not necessarily those with the best technical abilities.</li><li><strong>Scalability:</strong> For larger organizations, vibe coding may not scale well as an interview practice. It’s resource-intensive and might not fit into high-volume hiring processes.</li></ul><h3 id="finally"><strong>Finally</strong></h3><p><strong>Vibe coding is a tool, not a replacement.</strong> It offers a new perspective on candidate evaluation, but it’s <strong>not a silver bullet</strong>. Ultimately, hiring decisions are about <strong>understanding people</strong>—their skills, mindset, potential, and cultural alignment. The goal should be to <strong>blend the old and the new</strong>, not to abandon proven practices in favor of the latest trend.</p><p>A thoughtful, <strong>multi-dimensional interview process</strong> will always beat a one-size-fits-all approach.</p><h3 id="support-us">Support Us</h3>
