<p>SSH disconnections are one of those small but frustrating issues that can easily interrupt your workflow. You're typing, running a long job, or just leaving a terminal open for a while — then suddenly everything freezes and the connection drops. Most of the time, this happens because of <strong>idle timeouts</strong> enforced either by the SSH server, the network in between, or even your own device.</p><p>This article explains, in a clear and practical way, how SSH timeouts work, why they happen, and how to configure your server so that your session stays alive for <strong>about five minutes</strong> of inactivity (or longer, if you choose). It also covers important considerations that many people overlook.</p><hr><h2 id="why-ssh-sessions-time-out"><strong>Why SSH Sessions Time Out</strong></h2><p>At a high level, an SSH connection can be terminated because of three major factors:</p><ol><li><strong>Server-side inactivity rules</strong><br>Most systems include SSH daemon parameters such as <code>ClientAliveInterval</code> and <code>ClientAliveCountMax</code>. If they’re not set correctly, the server will not send any keepalive messages, and your session may die quickly when idle.</li><li><strong>Network or firewall behavior</strong><br>VPNs, corporate firewalls, cloud NAT gateways, and even some home routers aggressively remove idle TCP connections. If no packets flow for 30–60 seconds, they assume the session is dead.</li><li><strong>Client-side timeouts</strong><br>Your SSH client may also have its own keepalive mechanisms. If these aren't enabled, it won’t try to revive an idle connection.</li></ol><p>Understanding these layers helps you tune your setup to achieve the stability you want.</p><hr><h2 id="configuring-ssh-for-a-five-minute-idle-timeout"><strong>Configuring SSH for a Five-Minute Idle Timeout</strong></h2><p>If you want your SSH session to remain alive for <strong>approximately 5 minutes</strong>, the most reliable approach is configuring the server to send keepalive messages at predictable intervals.</p><p>Edit your SSH daemon configuration:</p><pre><code>sudo nano /etc/ssh/sshd_config
</code></pre><p>Set:</p><pre><code>ClientAliveInterval 60
ClientAliveCountMax 5
</code></pre><h3 id="what-these-settings-mean"><strong>What These Settings Mean</strong></h3><ul><li><strong>ClientAliveInterval 60</strong><br>The server will send a keepalive packet every <strong>60 seconds</strong> to ensure the session is still active.</li><li><strong>ClientAliveCountMax 5</strong><br>If the client fails to respond five times in a row, the server will terminate the session.</li></ul><p>Together:</p><pre><code>60 seconds × 5 = 300 seconds
→ exactly 5 minutes of tolerated inactivity
</code></pre><p>This configuration is stable, predictable, and widely used in production environments.</p><p>After editing the file:</p><pre><code>sudo systemctl restart sshd
</code></pre><hr><h2 id="situations-where-you-need-a-more-aggressive-approach"><strong>Situations Where You Need a More Aggressive Approach</strong></h2><p>In some environments — especially cloud platforms — you may discover that connections still die too quickly. This usually happens because NAT gateways or firewalls drop idle connections after <strong>30 seconds</strong> of inactivity.</p><p>If that’s your situation, adjust the settings to send keepalives more frequently:</p><pre><code>ClientAliveInterval 30
ClientAliveCountMax 10
</code></pre><p>This still gives you a <strong>five-minute window</strong>, but with check-ins every 30 seconds, making it less likely for external network devices to prematurely terminate the session.</p><hr><h2 id="strengthening-the-client-side-configuration"><strong>Strengthening the Client-Side Configuration</strong></h2><p>Although server-side settings do most of the work, you can improve stability by instructing the SSH client to send its own heartbeat signals.</p><p>Edit:</p><pre><code>nano ~/.ssh/config
</code></pre><p>Add:</p><pre><code>Host *
    ServerAliveInterval 60
    ServerAliveCountMax 5
</code></pre><p>Client keepalives help especially if:</p><ul><li>You often connect from laptops on Wi-Fi</li><li>You're behind corporate networks</li><li>You switch networks mid-session</li></ul><p>These small touches can significantly reduce disconnects.</p><hr><h2 id="using-tmux-or-screen-for-long-running-sessions"><strong>Using tmux or screen for Long-Running Sessions</strong></h2><p>Even with optimal timeout settings, you should never rely solely on SSH for protecting long-running processes. Tools like <strong>tmux</strong> and <strong>screen</strong> allow your session to persist even if your SSH connection drops unexpectedly.</p><p>Start a session:</p><pre><code>tmux
</code></pre><p>Then attach again if disconnected:</p><pre><code>tmux attach
</code></pre><p>This is essential for:</p><ul><li>Background jobs</li><li>Deployments</li><li>Database imports</li><li>Remote editing</li></ul><p>It prevents losing work due to network instability.</p><hr><h2 id="other-important-considerations-you-may-be-missing"><strong>Other Important Considerations You May Be Missing</strong></h2><h3 id="1-systemd-idle-timeouts"><strong>1. Systemd Idle Timeouts</strong></h3><p>Some distributions enforce additional idle rules beyond SSH. If the OS itself kills idle sessions, you must override <code>logind.conf</code>.</p><h3 id="2-vpn-behavior"><strong>2. VPN Behavior</strong></h3><p>Corporate VPNs often enforce strict idle thresholds. Keepalives help, but sometimes the VPN will still enforce hard limits.</p><h3 id="3-power-settings-on-laptops"><strong>3. Power Settings on Laptops</strong></h3><p>If your Wi-Fi card enters low-power mode, it may drop the connection. This can feel like an SSH timeout even though the server is fine.</p><h3 id="4-cloud-environment-quirks"><strong>4. Cloud Environment Quirks</strong></h3><p>AWS, Azure, and GCP NAT timeouts differ. Some aggressively kill idle connections unless packets are exchanged every 15–30 seconds.</p><h3 id="5-multi-hop-ssh"><strong>5. Multi-Hop SSH</strong></h3><p>If you use <code>ProxyJump</code> or SSH tunnels, remember each layer (client → jump host → destination) may require its own keepalive tuning.</p><hr><h2 id="finally"><strong>Finally</strong></h2><p>To maintain an SSH session for <strong>five minutes of idle time</strong>, the most reliable configuration is:</p><ul><li><strong>For true stability:</strong> use <strong>tmux</strong> so your work continues even if SSH disconnects.</li></ul><p><strong>If your network is unstable or cloud NAT is aggressive:</strong></p><pre><code>ClientAliveInterval 30
ClientAliveCountMax 10
</code></pre><p><strong>Client-side (optional):</strong></p><pre><code>ServerAliveInterval 60
ServerAliveCountMax 5
</code></pre><p><strong>Server-side:</strong></p><pre><code>ClientAliveInterval 60
ClientAliveCountMax 5
</code></pre><p>By understanding how SSH timeouts work and tuning both sides, you create a far more reliable and predictable remote-working environment — one where unexpected disconnects stop being part of your daily routine.</p><h3 id="support-us">Support Us</h3>
