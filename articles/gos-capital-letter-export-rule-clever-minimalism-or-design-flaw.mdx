<p>In the world of programming languages, <strong>Go (Golang)</strong> is often praised for its simplicity and pragmatism. Its creators stripped away much of the traditional complexity found in other languages, opting for a minimalist design. But with minimalism comes trade-offs, and one of the most debated ones is <strong>Go's rule that determines export visibility based on the capitalization of identifiers</strong>.</p><p>In Go, if you want a function, variable, struct, or constant to be visible outside its package, you <strong>capitalize its name</strong>. Otherwise, it remains <strong>unexported and private to the package</strong>.</p><pre><code class="language-go">// Exported
func PublicFunction() {}

// Not exported
func privateFunction() {}
</code></pre><p>At first glance, this might seem clever — elegant, even. But spend a bit of time in the Go ecosystem, and you’ll notice a common refrain:</p><blockquote>“This feels weird.”</blockquote><h3 id="why-many-developers-dislike-it"><strong>Why Many Developers Dislike It</strong></h3><h4 id="1-it%E2%80%99s-not-explicit"><strong>1. It’s Not Explicit</strong></h4><p>Developers coming from languages like Java, C#, or Rust are used to clear access modifiers like <code>public</code>, <code>private</code>, <code>protected</code>, or <code>pub(crate)</code>. In Go, <strong>visibility is inferred by casing</strong>. This goes against the grain of languages that value <strong>self-documenting, explicit code</strong>. You can’t quickly glance at a function and know its access level without understanding this convention.</p><h4 id="2-it-can-lead-to-naming-awkwardness"><strong>2. It Can Lead to Naming Awkwardness</strong></h4><p>When casing controls access, it introduces problems with <strong>naming consistency</strong>. For instance, acronyms like <code>HTTP</code> or <code>ID</code> often look awkward when capitalized:</p><pre><code class="language-go">type HTTPServer struct {}  // OK
type HttpServer struct {}  // Looks better, but less idiomatic in Go
</code></pre><p>Go's style guide recommends keeping acronyms in all-caps, but that can result in visually noisy code when everything starts with something like <code>UID</code>, <code>HTTP</code>, or <code>URL</code>.</p><h4 id="3-there%E2%80%99s-no-fine-grained-access-control"><strong>3. There’s No Fine-Grained Access Control</strong></h4><p>Go has <strong>no concept of internal, protected, or module-private visibility</strong>. You can either export to the entire world or keep it strictly inside the package. This <strong>limits encapsulation strategies</strong> in larger codebases, especially in enterprise-level applications.</p><h4 id="4-it%E2%80%99s-easy-to-miss"><strong>4. It’s Easy to Miss</strong></h4><p>Beginners often <strong>miss the export rule entirely</strong>, leading to bugs or confusion when a seemingly “valid” function isn’t accessible outside the package. There’s nothing in the syntax that screams "this is private" — just a lowercase letter.</p><hr><h3 id="why-some-defend-it"><strong>Why Some Defend It</strong></h3><p>Despite the criticism, Go’s visibility rule isn’t without merit.</p><h4 id="1-simplicity-and-convention"><strong>1. Simplicity and Convention</strong></h4><p>By replacing access modifiers with casing conventions, Go <strong>eliminates a whole category of keywords</strong> and reduces noise in the language. The convention is consistent and enforced, which keeps the language surface small.</p><h4 id="2-tooling-gets-it-right"><strong>2. Tooling Gets It Right</strong></h4><p>Because the rule is so predictable, tools like <code>go doc</code>, <code>gopls</code>, and IDEs can easily and accurately determine what’s exported. It’s <strong>mechanically simple</strong>, which aligns with Go’s philosophy of tool-driven development.</p><h4 id="3-encourages-small-packages"><strong>3. Encourages Small Packages</strong></h4><p>Some Go developers argue that the lack of access granularity encourages better modularization. You’re nudged to create <strong>small, focused packages</strong> with tight responsibilities — a design ideal in many clean code philosophies.</p><hr><h3 id="so-clever-or-flawed"><strong>So... Clever or Flawed?</strong></h3><p>Like much of Go’s design, the capitalization export rule is <strong>a trade-off between simplicity and control</strong>.</p><ul><li>If you value <strong>minimalist syntax</strong>, fewer keywords, and a flat learning curve, the rule might feel refreshing.</li><li>If you prefer <strong>explicit declarations</strong>, clearer boundaries, and nuanced visibility control, it might feel primitive or restrictive.</li></ul><p>In truth, <strong>Go forces you to think differently</strong>. Whether that’s a good thing depends on your background and the complexity of the software you’re building.</p><hr><h3 id="finally"><strong>Finally</strong></h3><p>Go's export convention is one of those "small things" that can stir strong opinions. It’s neither the most elegant nor the most terrible feature — but it’s a <strong>telling example of Go’s bold trade-offs</strong>. Like many Go idioms, it favors the <strong>machine and the team over the individual developer’s preferences</strong>.</p><p>In the end, <strong>you either learn to love it, live with it — or switch languages.</strong></p><h3 id="support-us">Support Us</h3>
