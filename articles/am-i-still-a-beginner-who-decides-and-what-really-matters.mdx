<p>One of the quiet questions we carry around in tech (and frankly, in most skill-driven fields) is:<br><strong>"When am I no longer a beginner?"</strong></p><p>It doesn’t come with a badge. No official memo lands in your inbox. You just sort of look up one day and wonder if you’ve quietly crossed that invisible line.</p><p>But who decides?<br><strong>You? Your manager? Your imposter syndrome?</strong> Let's break it down.</p><hr><h2 id="1-you-%E2%80%94-the-reluctant-judge"><strong>1. You — The Reluctant Judge</strong></h2><p>The irony is, <strong>you’re often the last person to realize</strong> you’ve moved past beginner status.</p><p>Why?</p><p>Because you’ve seen all the behind-the-scenes mess:</p><ul><li>The early failed attempts.</li><li>The half-baked PRs.</li><li>The Google searches that felt too basic.</li><li>The late nights debugging what turned out to be a typo.</li></ul><p>So when people praise your current skills, it feels undeserved. You’re <strong>keenly aware of what you don’t know</strong>. You compare your code to people five years ahead of you. And you're still uncomfortable owning the title “mid-level” or “senior” because it feels like a lie.</p><p>But here’s the catch:</p><blockquote><strong>Not feeling ready is often a sign that you’re leveling up.</strong> Beginners rarely worry about not knowing enough—<strong>that anxiety belongs to the competent</strong>.</blockquote><hr><h2 id="2-your-manager-%E2%80%94-the-one-with-a-checklist"><strong>2. Your Manager — The One With a Checklist</strong></h2><p>Most managers don’t tell you the exact moment you’re no longer a beginner, but they act like it:</p><ul><li>They assign you more complex tickets.</li><li>They stop checking your work line by line.</li><li>They trust your judgment on architecture.</li><li>They ask you to mentor interns or juniors.</li></ul><p>Managers often <strong>evaluate based on impact and independence</strong>. If you:</p><ul><li><strong>Own problems end to end</strong>,</li><li>Make good technical trade-offs,</li><li>Communicate risks clearly,</li><li>Don’t need constant direction...</li></ul><p>Then in their eyes, <strong>you’re definitely not a beginner anymore.</strong></p><p>You might still be growing. You might still ask a lot of questions. But your value to the team has shifted.</p><hr><h2 id="3-imposter-syndrome-%E2%80%94-the-voice-that-moves-the-goalpost"><strong>3. Imposter Syndrome — The Voice That Moves the Goalpost</strong></h2><p>Even if you’ve shipped features, optimized queries, resolved production bugs, or designed architecture, imposter syndrome whispers:</p><ul><li><em>“Real developers understand everything deeply.”</em></li><li><em>“You're just lucky no one’s caught on yet.”</em></li><li><em>“You’re not good—you’re just fast at Googling.”</em></li></ul><p>Imposter syndrome is <strong>not a reliable source</strong> of truth. It’s reactive, often emotional, and rooted in fear.</p><blockquote><strong>The more you care about quality and craft, the more likely you’ll feel like an imposter.</strong> Ironically, true beginners rarely suffer imposter syndrome—they don't know enough yet to doubt themselves.</blockquote><hr><h2 id="4-other-signs-you%E2%80%99re-not-a-beginner-anymore"><strong>4. Other Signs You’re Not a Beginner Anymore</strong></h2><p>Let’s zoom out. Forget titles and feelings. Here are <strong>objective signs</strong> you’ve crossed into intermediate or even advanced territory:</p><ul><li><strong>You know when to write less code.</strong><br>Beginners tend to prove skill through complexity. Experienced devs simplify.</li><li><strong>You make design decisions deliberately.</strong><br>You don’t just reach for tools; you choose them <em>because</em> they’re right for the problem.</li><li><strong>You prioritize maintainability over cleverness.</strong></li><li><strong>You understand trade-offs.</strong><br>Performance vs readability, abstraction vs speed, DRY vs simplicity.</li><li><strong>You mentor others.</strong><br>Even if you're not a “senior,” guiding someone else is a sign of maturity.</li><li><strong>You anticipate failure modes.</strong><br>Think: “What happens if this breaks?” or “How do we recover from this?”</li><li><strong>You write code with others in mind.</strong><br>Future-you. Teammates. Open source contributors. It shows up in your naming, structure, and comments.</li><li><strong>You debug effectively.</strong><br>Not just asking for help—but forming hypotheses, isolating issues, tracing logs. That’s real experience.</li></ul><hr><h2 id="5-so-who-should-decide"><strong>5. So, Who <em>Should</em> Decide?</strong></h2><p>The most honest answer: <strong>no one decides alone</strong>.</p><p>It’s a <strong>combination of self-awareness, external validation, and internal growth</strong>. Here’s how that might look:</p>

<hr><h2 id="6-final-considerations"><strong>6. Final Considerations</strong></h2><ul><li><strong>Titles vary wildly.</strong> A "senior" at a startup might be an intermediate elsewhere. Don't cling too tightly to labels.</li><li><strong>Being advanced doesn’t mean being perfect.</strong> Even top engineers ask for help. What changes is how they <strong>approach</strong> problems.</li><li><strong>Learning never stops.</strong> The beginner–intermediate–advanced spectrum is not linear. You might be advanced in backend, but a beginner in DevOps or security.</li><li><strong>Reflection matters.</strong> Journaling your progress, documenting wins, or reviewing your Git history helps you <em>see</em> the evolution that feelings often hide.</li></ul><hr><h2 id="finally-the-shift-is-subtle-but-powerful"><strong>Finally: The Shift Is Subtle but Powerful</strong></h2><p>You stop being a beginner <strong>not when you know everything</strong>, but when you start:</p><ul><li><strong>Making decisions intentionally</strong></li><li><strong>Writing for others, not just yourself</strong></li><li><strong>Recognizing patterns and avoiding common traps</strong></li><li><strong>Balancing depth with pragmatism</strong></li></ul><p>So if you’re wondering whether you’re still a beginner… you’re probably not.</p><p>Because <strong>beginners don’t ask that question.</strong></p><h3 id="support-us">Support Us</h3>
