<p>When <strong>Alan Kay</strong> coined the term <em>object-oriented programming</em> (OOP), his vision wasn’t about classes, inheritance, or design patterns. It was about <strong>independent objects</strong> communicating via <strong>message passing</strong>, much like biological cells or actors in a system. Yet, somehow, that idea got buried beneath layers of <strong>class hierarchies</strong>, <strong>composition</strong>, and <strong>method overloading</strong>.</p><p>So what happened? Why did <strong>message-passing OOP</strong>, the original ideal, get sidelined? And what can we learn from its journey?</p><hr><h2 id="the-original-vision-objects-as-independent-agents"><strong>The Original Vision: Objects as Independent Agents</strong></h2><p>Alan Kay’s inspiration came from biology and Lisp. Objects were supposed to be <strong>self-contained</strong>, capable of <strong>maintaining their own state</strong>, and <strong>interacting only through messages</strong>. These weren’t “bags of data with methods”—they were <strong>mini-programs</strong>, processing requests in their own way, possibly in parallel, and capable of <strong>evolving independently</strong>.</p><p>In short, OOP was meant to <strong>model behavior</strong>, not just structure.</p><hr><h2 id="why-the-vision-didn%E2%80%99t-catch-on"><strong>Why the Vision Didn’t Catch On</strong></h2><h3 id="1-hardware-realities-and-performance-constraints"><strong>1. Hardware Realities and Performance Constraints</strong></h3><p>In the 1970s and 80s, computing power was limited. Message-passing systems were <strong>slower and more memory-hungry</strong> than direct method calls on class instances. Meanwhile, languages like <strong>C++</strong> prioritized <strong>performance and determinism</strong>, offering a “good enough” version of OOP that fit neatly into the <strong>imperative, stack-based paradigm</strong> of the time.</p><p>The result? Message passing was <strong>seen as too abstract</strong>, too slow, and too different.</p><hr><h3 id="2-class-based-oop-was-easier-to-teach-and-learn"><strong>2. Class-Based OOP Was Easier to Teach and Learn</strong></h3><p>For most developers (and universities), it was easier to teach students that a “<strong>Dog is an Animal</strong>” than to explain asynchronous messaging between agents. <strong>Inheritance trees</strong>, <strong>object models</strong>, and <strong>method binding</strong> were concrete and visual.</p><p>Message passing, by contrast, was <strong>conceptual</strong> and often <strong>runtime-bound</strong>, making it feel more like dynamic magic than engineering.</p><hr><h3 id="3-the-languages-that-popularized-oop-took-a-different-path"><strong>3. The Languages That Popularized OOP Took a Different Path</strong></h3><p><strong>Smalltalk</strong>, the most faithful implementation of Kay’s vision, never became mainstream. Instead, <strong>Java, C++, and later C#</strong>—which all leaned heavily on <strong>class-based OOP</strong>—became the norm in enterprise, education, and tooling.</p><p>These languages emphasized <strong>encapsulation and polymorphism</strong>, but the messaging aspect was <strong>either hidden (method calls)</strong> or <strong>nonexistent</strong>.</p><hr><h3 id="4-tooling-and-ide-ecosystems-rewarded-class-centric-design"><strong>4. Tooling and IDE Ecosystems Rewarded Class-Centric Design</strong></h3><p>Static languages enabled <strong>powerful tooling</strong>: autocomplete, static analysis, refactoring tools, and early compilers. These required <strong>rigid structure and predictability</strong>. Message-passing models, often <strong>dynamic and late-bound</strong>, made these tools harder to build.</p><p>In other words: IDEs loved Java. They <strong>struggled with Smalltalk or Lisp-like flexibility</strong>.</p><hr><h3 id="5-message-passing-wasnt-urgently-needed"><strong>5. Message Passing Wasn't Urgently Needed</strong></h3><p>Most early applications were <strong>single-threaded</strong>, running on a single machine. The <strong>concurrency and fault tolerance benefits</strong> of message passing were overkill for desktop GUIs, CRUD apps, or early web servers.</p><p>It wasn’t until later—when <strong>distributed systems</strong>, <strong>multi-core CPUs</strong>, and <strong>asynchronous UIs</strong> became the norm—that the strengths of message passing started to matter again.</p><hr><h2 id="what-we-might-have-missed"><strong>What We Might Have Missed</strong></h2><p>The overemphasis on classes and inheritance led to some <strong>problematic patterns</strong>:</p><ul><li><strong>Tight coupling</strong> between classes</li><li><strong>Rigid hierarchies</strong> that were hard to change</li><li><strong>Inheritance abuse</strong>, where behavior was inherited that shouldn’t have been</li><li><strong>The fragile base class problem</strong></li><li>Lack of <strong>natural concurrency</strong> and <strong>resilience</strong></li></ul><p>The message-passing model, by contrast, naturally leads to <strong>loose coupling</strong>, <strong>composability</strong>, and <strong>fault isolation</strong>—all things we rediscovered later with <strong>actor models</strong>, <strong>microservices</strong>, and <strong>event-driven architectures</strong>.</p><hr><h2 id="echoes-of-the-original-vision-in-modern-systems"><strong>Echoes of the Original Vision in Modern Systems</strong></h2><p>Interestingly, the <strong>message-passing paradigm never truly died</strong>—it just rebranded:</p><ul><li><strong>Erlang/Elixir’s actor model</strong> thrives in telecoms and distributed systems</li><li><strong>Akka</strong> brought the actor model to the JVM</li><li><strong>Frontend frameworks</strong> (like React with Redux or Signals) emphasize <strong>event-driven state</strong></li><li><strong>Microservices</strong> talk to each other via <strong>asynchronous messages</strong></li><li>Even <strong>JavaScript</strong> with its <strong>event loop and promises</strong> leans toward this direction</li></ul><p>We're slowly circling back to Kay’s ideas—just under different names.</p><hr><h2 id="what-the-future-might-hold"><strong>What the Future Might Hold</strong></h2><p>As systems become more <strong>distributed</strong>, <strong>concurrent</strong>, and <strong>resilient</strong>, the limitations of class-based OOP become more apparent. Message-passing models are making a comeback, particularly in:</p><ul><li><strong>Cloud-native systems</strong></li><li><strong>Reactive programming</strong></li><li><strong>IoT</strong></li><li><strong>Edge computing</strong></li></ul><p>With <strong>WebAssembly</strong>, <strong>actors on the edge</strong>, and <strong>decentralized systems</strong>, the need for <strong>self-contained agents</strong> that talk via messages is clearer than ever.</p><hr><h2 id="finally"><strong>Finally</strong></h2><p>The original vision of OOP as <strong>message-passing between autonomous agents</strong> didn’t fail—it was simply ahead of its time. The world wasn’t ready for it in the 70s or 90s. But now, in a world of <strong>distributed apps, microservices, and real-time systems</strong>, we’re seeing that vision return—this time with the computing power, networking, and tooling to make it work.</p><p>Perhaps, in the end, <strong>message-passing didn’t lose the war</strong>—it just took a longer route to win it.</p><h3 id="support-us">Support Us</h3>
